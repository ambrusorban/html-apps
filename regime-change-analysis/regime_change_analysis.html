Comparative Reading · Five Hungarian Essays on Rendszerváltás
Editorial Review  ·  April 2026

Rendszerváltás reconsidered

Five Hungarian commentators — a sociologist, a political philosopher, a veteran journalist, a rural intellectual, and an empirical researcher — converge on an uncomfortable claim: the language of "regime change" no longer describes anything that is actually changing. This page maps where their analyses overlap, where they diverge, and what they together tell us about the legacy of 1989 and the meaning of 2026.

Mérce18 May 2021  ·  Empirical Research

The Victims of Hopelessness: What Do We Know About the Post-1989 Excess Mortality of Seven Million?

By Gábor Scheiring · sociologist, originally in Új Egyenlőség

Drawing on the PrivMort research project, Scheiring argues that the speed and brutality of privatisation and de-industrialisation — not "post-Soviet drinking culture" or health-system collapse — were the determining cause of an estimated 7.3 million excess deaths across Eastern Europe between 1991 and 1999, roughly 5 million in Russia alone.

  • Mortality concentrated in working-age men (suicide, cardiac events, alcohol-related conditions) — a pattern of stress, not infrastructural failure.
  • In Hungary, c. 40% of industrial jobs vanished between 1988 and 1995 — deindustrialisation "unparalleled globally".
  • Cities that retained state ownership showed significantly lower mortality than rapidly privatised ones; a strong welfare buffer reduced damage in Hungary versus Russia.
  • Frames the catastrophe through Durkheim's anomie and Engels's structural violence: deaths-of-despair are political, not cultural.

One of the greatest demographic catastrophes in recent history outside of famines and wars — produced by policy choices, not destiny.

Substack · Politika / filozófia1 Apr 2026  ·  Political Philosophy

Rendszerváltás

By Kapelner Zsolt · political philosopher

Kapelner argues that "regime change" has become a slogan emptied of content. After sixteen years of Orbán, Hungarian discourse has produced no serious answer to the question of what democracy should actually deliver — making the opposition vulnerable to declaring any non-Fidesz outcome a victory of principle.

  • Distinguishes kormányváltás (government change) from rendszerváltás: the latter requires a structural redistribution of power that the opposition has neither defined nor debated.
  • Warns of a "permanent regime-change" cycle, where each side warps institutions in office — converging on Western competitive elite politics rather than escaping it.
  • Notes that the historical "West" Hungarians once aimed to join is itself collapsing into "neo-fascism and reactionary centrism": Trump's US, militarising Europe, Gaza, Mediterranean refugee camps.
  • Closes with cautious pessimism: voters cannot counterbalance elite capture of systemic transformation, though he hopes to be proved wrong.

Nothing remains from the Enlightenment's promise — destroyed in US neo-imperial wars, Mediterranean refugee camps, Gaza.

Mérce9 Apr 2026  ·  Historical-Sociological Critique

Regime Change 2.0: Proclaiming a New Transition Without Reckoning With the Burdens of the Last One

By Dániel Szabó · rural-based intellectual

Szabó treats the Tisza Party's "Regime Change 2.0" as historical amnesia. The opposition campaigns on rule-of-law restoration while refusing to confront the unresolved costs of 1989: two million lost jobs, a neoliberal experimental laboratory, and a peripheral economy hostage to German automotive cycles.

  • Hungary, unlike Poland, accepted IMF conditionality without debt forgiveness and built growth on suppressed wages and FDI — "growth that produced no jobs" for 25 years.
  • Acknowledges Fidesz's genuine employment gains (~4.1m → 4.7m workers by 2022) as a real material basis for its rural support — not mere propaganda susceptibility.
  • But Fidesz preserved neoliberalism while adding authoritarianism, weakened labour law, and rotated oligarchs — not transforming the structure.
  • Tisza's leadership of multinational executives signals continuity, not rupture: another personnel change dressed as systemic change.

They proclaim a new system change while refusing to reckon even with the heavy burdens and grave consequences of the last one.

Mérce16 Apr 2026  ·  Op-Ed

It Could Happen for the Tenth Time — If We Don't Finally Face Reality

By Kabai Domokos Lajos · journalist (b. 1951)

Writing days after Tisza's victory of 12 April 2026, Kabai argues that the post-election cycle of celebration and disillusionment will repeat for the tenth time unless Hungarians acknowledge that capitalism itself — not merely "this government" — is what shapes their lives.

  • The chants of "Ria, ria, Hungária!" sounded indistinguishable on both sides: a giveaway that what changes is the team, not the field.
  • The new ruling stratum simply felt under-rewarded by Orbán; the same beneficiaries of segregation, flat tax and clientelism are now repositioning.
  • Hungary's troubles are pan-European: capital flows westward, the EU's share of world output is shrinking, and AI plus sovereign debt will accelerate that decline.
  • "System-change" rhetoric without system-change policy is propaganda that deliberately empties words like corruption, democracy and Europeanness.

If we do not finally face reality, what has happened nine times after every Hungarian election can happen again for the tenth.

Mérce23 Apr 2026  ·  World-Systems / Marxist Analysis

On the Self-Destructive Lies Repeated Around the Change of Government

By Csányi Gergely · sociologist, Helyzet Műhely

Csányi names three "self-destructive lies" the Hungarian left is telling itself after Tisza's win: that a regime change occurred, that "returning to the West" is liberation, and that Orbán has been totally defeated. Each, he argues, narrows political imagination and guarantees the return of right-wing populism.

  • The capitalist economic-social system is intact; only the administration changed. Calling it rendszerváltás imports a false sense of irreversibility.
  • The "West" of 2026 — ICE, Frontex, Gaza, German far-right surge, NATO in crisis — is not a destination; "there is no East and West", only US-China hegemonic struggle.
  • Orbán won the deeper battle: the left now celebrates a right-wing technocrat's victory and reproduces NER's "us-or-them, no third option" frame.
  • The task is to use the widened political space to build anti-systemic institutions before the next crisis — this is not a Belle Époque, instability is the near future.

The right's deepest victory is that the left now has to celebrate one — on its terms.

02  /  Recurring Topics

What the essays orbit around

Eight clusters of concern surface across the five texts. Each appears in at least three of the five, often in all five.

Concept

The meaning of rendszerváltás

What counts as a system change — and what does the opposition mean by claiming to deliver one? All five authors treat the term as either misused or empty.

Structure

Capitalism's continuity

The shared diagnosis: the underlying capitalist economic order persists across both the 1989 transition and the 2026 election; only personnel and rhetoric rotate.

History

The unsettled legacy of 1989

Mass job loss, hollowed welfare, demographic damage, and dependency on FDI — treated by Scheiring, Szabó, Kabai and Csányi as the still-unprocessed event.

Geopolitics

The decline of the "West"

Trump, Gaza, Frontex, German far-right surge, EU's shrinking share of world output: the West Hungarians once aspired to is no longer the West they would arrive at.

Periphery

Hungary's structural dependency

An open economy hinged on German automotive cycles, IMF conditionality, suppressed wages: peripheral integration as the underlying condition of Hungarian politics.

Politics

The opposition's evasions

Tisza promises rule-of-law restoration and anti-corruption while avoiding constitutional reform, electoral-system reform, or a break with international capital.

Cycle

Disillusionment as pattern

Kabai names it explicitly — ten elections, nine disillusionments. Csányi, Szabó and Kapelner share the worry that the structural conditions for Orbánism remain.

Method

Despair as political fact

From Scheiring's mortality data to Csányi's "no Belle Époque" warning, despair is treated not as a mood but as a measurable, policy-driven outcome.

03  /  Where the Authors Meet and Part

Agreements & differences

Two columns: shared theses across the five essays on the left, the most consequential disagreements and shifts of emphasis on the right.

Where they agree

The 1989 transition was structurally costly.

All five authors treat the post-1989 settlement as having produced lasting damage: deindustrialisation, mass unemployment, weakened welfare, peripheral dependency, and (in Scheiring's reading) demographic catastrophe. None treat it as a clean success story to be repeated.

"Regime change" is being misused in 2026.

The four 2026 essays converge: calling Tisza's victory a rendszerváltás imports a sense of finality and depth that the actual programme cannot support. The structural order — economic, geopolitical, institutional — is not what changes.

KapelnerSzabóKabaiCsányi

Capitalism — not the governing party — is the operative system.

From Scheiring's privatisation research to Csányi's world-systems framing, the underlying claim is the same: the lives of Hungarians are shaped primarily by their position in a global capitalist economy that successive governments have administered, not transformed.

ScheiringSzabóKabaiCsányi

The "West" is no longer a credible normative anchor.

Three of the four 2026 authors argue the historical promise of joining "the West" has dissolved into Trumpism, militarised Europe, Frontex and Gaza. Liberal rhetoric of "returning to Europe" is read as anachronistic.

KapelnerKabaiCsányi

The opposition's silence on structure is itself the problem.

All four 2026 authors see the same evasion: an opposition that promises restoration and competence but not constitutional, electoral or political-economic transformation — reproducing the very conditions that produced Orbán in the first place.

KapelnerSzabóKabaiCsányi

Without structural reckoning, the cycle resumes.

Whether named as "the tenth time" (Kabai), "permanent regime change" (Kapelner), or the inevitable return of right-wing populism (Csányi, Szabó), the prediction is shared: change of office without change of structure produces another disillusionment, and another opening for authoritarian politics.

Where they differ

Method & idiom.

The five voices speak in five registers: Scheiring brings quantitative epidemiology; Csányi a world-systems Marxism; Szabó a historical-sociological reconstruction; Kapelner a normative political philosophy; Kabai a journalistic warning in the tradition of the experienced commentator.

How much credit Fidesz deserves.

Szabó is the most willing to grant Fidesz real material achievements — the rise from c. 4.1m to 4.7m workers, particularly in rural areas, as the substantive basis of its support. Csányi and Kabai treat Fidesz's economy primarily as a continuation of neoliberalism dressed in nationalism; Kapelner is largely indifferent to the empirical record and focused on conceptual failure.

What "the West" actually is.

Kapelner sees the West as having betrayed its own Enlightenment promise — a tragic loss. Csányi argues the East/West binary no longer exists at all: there is only US-China hegemonic struggle. Kabai treats the West more soberly as an economically declining bloc. Szabó's frame is narrower: Hungary's specific neoliberal subordination to Western capital.

What politics should now do.

Csányi is the most strategic and prescriptive: build anti-systemic institutions in the widened post-NER space. Szabó wants opposition forces to confront 1989's legacy in their programme. Kabai is closer to diagnosis without prescription. Kapelner is the most pessimistic about agency — he doubts mass politics can counter elite capture. Scheiring, writing earlier, calls for evidence-based welfare and labour-market policy.

The role of the opposition voter.

Szabó takes Fidesz's voters seriously as responding to real material change; Kabai is more cynical about the symmetry of celebration on both sides; Csányi worries the left will internalise NER's "no third option" framing precisely by celebrating Tisza; Kapelner is structurally skeptical that any voter coalition can deliver more than elite rotation.

Tone toward the future.

Csányi's posture is combative-strategic — danger and opportunity together. Kabai is cynical-warning: the cycle will probably repeat. Kapelner is melancholically pessimistic. Szabó is analytic and demanding: the legacy must be confronted. Scheiring is scholarly and corrective: better policy is possible.

05  /  Synthesis

What the five essays say together

Read as a single conversation, the five essays describe a Hungarian left that has finally put the founding event of its present, the 1989 transition, on the analytical table — and is asking whether a 2026 election ought to be allowed to repeat the same trick under a new name.

Their shared diagnosis is that the operative regime in Hungary is not Fidesz, and will not be Tisza: it is the peripheral integration of a small open economy into a global capitalism whose Western pole is itself decaying. Within that frame, "regime change" is at best the wrong word and at worst a piece of self-deception that guarantees another cycle.

If the 1989 transition produced seven million excess deaths in our region, then "regime change" is not a word to be reused lightly — certainly not for a change of governing personnel that leaves the structure intact.

Their differences are differences of register, not of direction. Scheiring brings the receipts in mortality data. Szabó forces the opposition to look at a balance sheet it would rather skip. Kabai broadcasts the warning in a journalistic register honed over five decades. Kapelner asks the philosophical question the others assume: what would it even mean to succeed? Csányi sketches a strategy for the political space that has just opened.

Together they suggest that the most useful thing that could now happen in Hungarian politics is the one thing the new government is least likely to do: confront the previous regime change before declaring a new one.
