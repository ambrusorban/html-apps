Diagnosing the Hungarian Regime

A comparative reading of Endre Feigl and Schultz Nóra — two emerging public voices on illiberalism, hegemony, and the late phase of the Orbán system

1Introduction

Two writers, born of different intellectual traditions, have lately turned their attention to the same object: the Hungarian regime as it enters its third decade. Endre Feigl, writing under the byline Közéleti Paradoxon, approaches it as a sociologist building a theoretical model of illiberalism out of the materials of post-Marxist political theory. Schultz Nóra, trained at Cambridge and the LSE, approaches it as a political analyst diagnosing not only the regime but the failing antibodies around it.

This paper places their work side by side. It treats their writing as a single conversation taking place across separate Substack feeds — about what illiberalism is, what it does, and what comes after. The aim is not to adjudicate between them, but to surface what each sees, what each misses, and what their convergences and divergences tell us about the contemporary diagnosis of the Orbán system.

Author A

Endre Feigl

Sociologist · Közéleti Paradoxon

Feigl writes a deliberately scholarly Substack series. His method is the synthesis essay: marshalling political theorists — Gramsci, TGM, Jessop, James C. Scott — to produce a structural account of how illiberal systems are built, sustained, and resisted. His "Az illiberális rendszerek felépítése" series (currently three parts) reads as if drafted for a doctoral seminar before being lifted onto Substack.

Stance: regime-critical, but at one remove from day-to-day politics. His addressee is the educated reader who wants the mechanism, not the headline.

Author B

Schultz Nóra

Political analyst · podcaster · LSE / Cambridge

Schultz holds a degree in Human, Social and Political Sciences from Cambridge and an MSc in Political Theory from the LSE. Gold medallist of the 2017 Philosophy Student Olympiad. Five years as podcaster (Belépési Küszöb) and now an independent analyst with bylines in The Guardian, HVG, 24.hu, The Washington Post.

Stance: regime-critical, but unwilling to spare the opposition. Her Substack functions as a running diagnostic of both the system and its would-be challengers — what she has called the "ellenzéki cringe faktor."

3Feigl's Three Publications

I.

Az illiberális rendszerek felépítése I.

Published 9 April 2026 · Foundational essay of the series

Argument. Illiberal democracies are neither failed democracies nor recurrences of classical dictatorship. They preserve the formal architecture of democratic institutions while emptying out their substantive content — "the content of checks and balances, constitutional self-restraint, pluralistic competition and legal equality gradually empties out." Civil society is constrained not by terror but by resource starvation and administrative burden, producing what Feigl calls an asymmetric power environment.

Framework. Four-cornered theoretical foundation:

  • TGM's post-fascism — to distinguish contemporary authoritarianism from the historical variety.
  • Gramsci's hegemony — to show how domination operates through cultural consensus, not only coercion.
  • Bob Jessop's state theory — to analyse exceptional governance and state transformation.
  • James C. Scott's "weapons of the weak" — to model the transformed repertoires of resistance.

Conclusion. A serious analysis of illiberalism must run simultaneously across constitutional, political, economic, cultural, and social-movement registers. Resistance remains possible, but only in transfigured form.

II.

Az illiberális rendszerek felépítése II: TGM

Published 1 May 2026 · Deep reading of Tamás Gáspár Miklós

Argument. Post-fascism, after TGM, is "political systems, practices, routines and ideologies" that retain democratic institutional form while pursuing authoritarian organisation, comfortably nested inside global capitalism. Its central operation is the uncoupling of citizenship from human dignity — a reversal of the Enlightenment universalisation of the citizen.

Mechanism. The regime divides populations into the "homogeneous productive" (labour, order, reproduction, health) and the "heterogeneous expendable." For the latter, formal citizenship persists but is hollowed of substantive rights. Crucially, no dramatic violence is required; neglect suffices.

Citation density. Unusually for the series, this essay is built on a single foundational text: TGM (2000), On Post-Fascism, East European Constitutional Review. The piece is essentially an extended commentary.

III.

Az illiberális rendszerek felépítése III: A hibrid ellenforradalom kora

Published 19 May 2026 · Filippov and the mechanics of durability

Argument. Through Gábor Filippov's framework of the "hybrid counter-revolution," Feigl identifies five mechanisms that make modern illiberal regimes durable in a way 20th-century autocracies were not:

  1. Cost avoidance. Open repression is expensive; selective restriction is cheap.
  2. Institutional weaponisation. Democratic procedure generates the very compliance that suppresses resistance.
  3. Rhetorical capture. "Democratic and institutional slogans become the most effective weapons against democracy."
  4. Privatised violence. Enforcement outsourced to private actors, producing plausible deniability.
  5. Civil-society disruption. NGOs are not banned — they are buried under regulatory load.

Conclusion. The illiberal regime is a categorically new political form. Critics rightly diagnose democratic erosion; defenders rightly note that the formal freedoms remain. Both are correct, and the gap between them is the system itself. "Érdemes tehát résen lenni" — vigilance is warranted.

4Feigl — Themes, Continuities, Tensions

Continuities

Across the three essays Feigl is remarkably consistent about what illiberalism is not: it is not a deficient democracy waiting to recover, and it is not a soft dictatorship waiting to harden. It is a stable third form. He returns repeatedly to four moves:

  • Institutions are preserved as shells while their content is hollowed.
  • Power is exercised culturally and procedurally, not primarily coercively.
  • Citizenship and rights are formally retained, substantively withdrawn.
  • The regime is cost-rational: it chooses the cheapest viable form of control.

Tensions within the corpus

Tension 1 — Resistance vs. capture. Essay I closes on James C. Scott and the persistence of transfigured resistance. Essay III closes on the near-total capture of institutional, rhetorical, and civic space. The reader is left to reconcile a tradition that says "weapons of the weak always remain" with a model in which every register of resistance has been pre-emptively co-opted.

Tension 2 — Marxian post-fascism vs. institutionalist hybrid theory. Essay II builds the regime on the Marxist diagnosis (TGM): illiberalism is a phase of global capitalism, the dispossessed merely lack substantive citizenship. Essay III shifts to Filippov's institutionalist register, where the regime is built out of procedural choices and cost calculations. The two lenses do not contradict, but they do not quite meet either — capitalism barely appears in Essay III; institutional design barely appears in Essay II.

Tension 3 — Cultural hegemony vs. plausible deniability. Gramsci's hegemony presumes consent: the public actively accepts the regime's worldview. Filippov's privatised violence presumes concealment: the public is not asked to consent so much as denied the evidence. Feigl invokes both without fully resolving whether the Hungarian subject is a believer or a deniable victim.

Relation to his references

Feigl's relationship to his sources is genuinely interpretive rather than ornamental. TGM is not invoked as authority but extended: TGM's 2000 essay said little about the digital-procedural infrastructure that Filippov later analyses, and Feigl uses the latter to update the former. Gramsci is treated less as a Marxist forebear than as a method — hegemony as the analytical scalpel. Where the references show strain is in Scott: in a regime whose central trick is procedural capture, the "weapons of the weak" tradition (which presumes a sphere of everyday autonomy below the radar) sits awkwardly. Feigl gestures at it; he does not fully integrate it.

5Schultz's Recent Writings

A.

Elérkezett a dekadens Orbán-rendszer korszaka

14 May 2025 · The diagnosis of decadence

Argument. Borrowing Ross Douthat's framework from The Decadent Society (2020) and pairing it with Gramsci's "the old is dying and the new cannot be born," Schultz argues the Orbán regime has entered a decadent phase: not collapsing, but exhausted of dynamism.

Indicators. Objective: economic growth gone, demographic policy failed despite enormous outlay. Subjective: innovation poverty, recycled enemies (media, LGBTQ+, "intellectuals"), inability to generate viral cultural energy of the kind around Trump, "spiritual" emptiness of the regime's core narratives.

Twist. Decadence does not mean collapse. It produces "stronger and more aggressive clinging to power" — the regime becomes more dangerous as it ages, not less. The next election, she argues, is the preferable exit; the alternative is crisis or catastrophe.

B.

A Tisza egy politikai Rorschach-teszt mindannyiunknak

31 January 2026 · Opposition as projection screen

Argument. The Tisza movement resists existing political categories because observers project onto it. The piece extends her recurring trope of the "ellenzéki cringe faktor" — opposition acts that read as self-undermining (her examples include Tordai's poll commentary, MKKP's stunts, Hadházy's electoral system claims). She floats the term "ellenzéki delulu" as possibly more honest.

Method. Phenomenological: she reads the political object by reading the reactions to it. The Rorschach metaphor is doing real analytical work, not decorative.

C.

Az elitről az elitváltás küszöbén

2 March 2026 · A critique of Hungarian progressive elites

Argument. Drawing on her experience inside both British elite academia and Budapest progressive intellectual circles, Schultz argues that Hungarian progressive elites are caught in a self-reproducing pathology. The college seminar (szakkollégium) system rewards ideological correctness over impact. Career ambition is morally suspect. The crowning move is what she calls the "self-destructive glorification of failure" — "a sikertelenség önsorsrontó dicsőítése." Defeat is read as principle; principle becomes the alibi for political impotence.

Stake. If a regime change is approaching, the elite expected to replace the incumbents is structurally unfit to govern. This is the unstated stake behind the whole article.

6Schultz — Themes, Method, Voice

What recurs

  • The system is in late phase. Decadence, exhaustion, recycled enemies — a regime burning through its remaining legitimacy reserves.
  • The opposition is part of the diagnosis. Unusually for regime-critical writing, the would-be challengers receive as much scrutiny as the incumbents.
  • The elite question. Who is supposed to govern after Orbán? Schultz is quietly insistent that nobody has answered this.
  • The cultural-spiritual register. The regime fails not only economically but in producing meaning. Decadence is, ultimately, a category of style.

Method

Where Feigl synthesises theorists, Schultz reads situations. She is closer to the diagnostic essayist (Didion, whom she explicitly invokes elsewhere) than to the academic sociologist. Her references are eclectic and unexpectedly cross-ideological: Ross Douthat, a conservative-Catholic New York Times columnist, supplies the central framework of the decadence essay. Gramsci appears, but as a one-line aphorism, not as a method. The intellectual signature is wide reading, narrow citation.

Voice

The first-person presence is real and acknowledged ("my experience in British elite circles… in Budapest progressive milieus…"). She names limitations of her own vantage (limited familiarity with conservative governing elites and business circles). The writing is plainspoken, dryly funny, and willing to coin terms ("ellenzéki delulu") that would be unthinkable in a sociology journal.

7Thinkers Behind the Texts

Tamás Gáspár Miklós (TGM)

Hungarian-Romanian philosopher, "On Post-Fascism" (2000)

In Feigl: The keystone. Post-fascism is the conceptual primitive on which Essay II is built and to which Essays I and III return.

In Schultz: Not cited directly. The shared Hungarian intellectual horizon is felt rather than declared.

Antonio Gramsci

Italian Marxist, Prison Notebooks

In Feigl: A method — hegemony as the analytical key to how illiberal regimes secure consent rather than impose it.

In Schultz: An aphorism — "the old is dying and the new cannot be born" — used to frame decadence as an interregnum.

Gábor Filippov

Hungarian political scientist, "A hibrid ellenforradalom kora" (2018)

In Feigl: Provides the five-pillar model of illiberal durability that organises Essay III.

In Schultz: Absent. Filippov's procedural-institutional register is not her language.

Ross Douthat

American conservative columnist, The Decadent Society (2020)

In Feigl: Absent.

In Schultz: The central framework of her decadence essay. Notably cross-ideological — a conservative diagnosis recruited for a liberal-critical argument.

James C. Scott

American political scientist, Domination and the Arts of Resistance (1990/1992)

In Feigl: Cited in Essay I as the resource for theorising transfigured resistance under non-violent authoritarianism.

In Schultz: Not cited. Her question is not how to survive the regime but how to replace it.

Bob Jessop

British state theorist, The State: Past, Present, Future (2016)

In Feigl: Provides the apparatus for thinking about state transformation and exceptional governance.

In Schultz: Absent.

Bánkuti / Bozóki / Krekó / Wilkin

Hungarian-studies scholarship on the Fidesz regime, 2012–2022

In Feigl: Foundational empirical references for the regime-change story (constitutional overhaul, information autocracy).

In Schultz: Largely absent from the cited apparatus, though presupposed in the background.

Joan Didion

American essayist

In Feigl: Absent.

In Schultz: Cited as a model of how to write when public moral language has become unusable. The literary essayist is part of her toolkit.

9Synthesis

Where they meet

Both writers refuse the two dominant framings of the Hungarian regime. They reject the "deficient democracy" reading that treats Orbán's system as a recoverable variant of liberal democracy, and they reject the "dictatorship" reading that imports a 20th-century template. Both insist the regime is a new form. Both also reach for Gramsci — Feigl as method, Schultz as aphorism — to locate the regime in a moment of suspended transition.

Where they diverge

The deepest divergence is not about the regime but about what counts as analysis. For Feigl, analysis is the production of a theoretical model robust enough to hold across constitutional, cultural, and economic registers. The reader is being equipped with a lens. For Schultz, analysis is reading the room: noticing what the regime has stopped being able to do, what the opposition is doing to itself, what tone the public discourse is taking. The reader is being equipped with a posture.

The second divergence is about the timeline. Feigl's essays are written in the present tense of a stable phenomenon — illiberalism is here, vigilance is warranted. Schultz writes in the present tense of an ending — "They wouldn't take such steps if they didn't sense the end." If both are right, the implication is that the regime is at once structurally consolidated and dynamically exhausted; durable in form, declining in energy. This is in fact a coherent description, but neither author quite states it.

A productive contradiction

Read together, Feigl and Schultz produce an image that neither produces alone: an illiberal regime that has succeeded in building a durable institutional architecture (Feigl) and is simultaneously running out of the cultural fuel needed to inhabit it (Schultz). Feigl gives us the skeleton; Schultz tells us the body is tired. If there is a single working hypothesis to extract from putting them in conversation, it is this: the Hungarian system will outlast its energy.

What each is missing

Feigl's blind spot is the political-strategic dimension Schultz inhabits — the question of who actually replaces this, and whether the elite expected to do so is capable. He treats civil society as an abstract category; she treats it as a set of identifiable, named, sometimes embarrassing people.

Schultz's blind spot is the question Feigl makes central — why does the formal architecture matter. Her writing can occasionally treat the regime as if it were primarily an aesthetic and atmospheric phenomenon. Feigl reminds us that the constitutional and procedural cage is real, has been built deliberately, and will outlast Orbán himself.

Each, in other words, supplies the corrective the other needs.
